Scientific Background

AgroScan Report

An open-source platform for scalable soil organic carbon estimation — bridging remote sensing, machine learning, and sustainable agriculture.

Introduction

Soil Organic Carbon & Its Importance

Soils represent the largest terrestrial carbon pool globally and play a critical role as carbon sinks through soil carbon sequestration, where atmospheric CO₂ is stored as soil organic matter via photosynthesis [1]. Agricultural soils with high soil organic carbon (SOC) content deliver significant environmental and agronomic co- benefits: improved long-term productivity, increased biodiversity, and preservation of ecosystem services [2].

Consequently, monitoring and enhancing SOC stocks has become a priority within major policy frameworks, including the EU Soil Strategy for 2030 that translates into the EU Soil Monitoring Law [2][3]. Despite this recognized importance, farmers face substantial barriers when integrating SOC monitoring into their agricultural practices. Conventional soil sampling requires repeated field and laboratory work across extended time periods, creating significant cost, labor, and scalability constraints that limit routine application across large agricultural areas [4].

Machine learning-based remote sensing models have emerged as a promising approach to address these limitations. By leveraging satellite-derived data, these models offer scalable and cost-efficient SOC estimation and allow a baseline to be established using historic satellite imagery to assess the impact of sustainable soil management (SSM) practices over time. However, many existing tools remain restricted by paywalls, limited accessibility, or insufficient methodological transparency, constraining their adoption in both research and practice.

AgroScan addresses these gaps by providing a free, open-source platform that enables farmers to assess the SOC content of their fields, monitor the effectiveness of SSM practices, and engage with evidence when advocating to policymakers. As an open- source tool, it provides full transparency on the underlying methodology and allows users to personalize or extend the platform for their own use cases.

How can interactive machine learning be utilized to empower farmers to monitor and manage carbon stock in their agricultural soils?
Literature Review

Remote sensing + ML for SOC prediction

Nguyen et al. (2022)[6] demonstrated a multi-sensor data fusion model combining multispectral image data from Sentinel-2 and synthetic aperture radar data from Sentinel-1, achieving high prediction accuracy (R² = 0.870, RMSE = 1.818 t/ha). The study highlighted that incorporating derived predictor features — including vegetation and soil indices calculated from raw band values — significantly improved model performance.

Guo et al. (2021)[5] showed that Sentinel-2 optical data and crop-growth indices are particularly effective predictors for agricultural soils, which typically exhibit lower topological variability than other land use types. Similar results were demonstrated by Yuzugullu et al. (2024)[19], Beisekenov et al. (2025)[20], and Zhang et al. (2019)[21]. Taken together, these studies confirm that the technical foundation for remote sensing-based SOC prediction in agricultural contexts is well established.

Despite academic progress, accessible farmer-facing tools remain limited. Commercial tools are often paywalled and opaque, providing users little agency to validate or adapt methods to local contexts. These gaps — limited availability, lack of methodological transparency, and the absence of user agency — motivated this work.

Data methodology

Case region, sources, processing

1. Selection of case region
Germany was selected as the case region due to its significant agricultural sector, strong policy relevance, and high soil data availability. Around half of Germany’s land area is used for agriculture, making it one of the European Union’s largest agricultural producers (BMEL, 2023)[12]. Germany must comply with the European Union Soil Monitoring Law by 2028, increasing the demand for scalable and cost-effective soil monitoring methods (European Union, 2025)[3]. The LUCAS 2019 soil dataset has 332 agricultural sampling locations in Germany, of which 270 could be matched to Sentinel-2 data (Fernández-Ugalde et al., 2022)[22].

2. Data sources
Two publicly available and freely accessible datasets were selected as the foundation for the AgroScan model to support reproducibility and low barriers to adoption.

Input data: Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery (Copernicus)[13]. Level-2A Bottom-of-Atmosphere reflectance values. Selected model input bands: B2, B3, B4, B5, B6, B7, B8, B8A, B11, B12.

Target variable: LUCAS Topsoil dataset (0–20 cm)[15], filtered to cropland samples; harmonized EU-wide ground truth.

3. Data collection and processing
The pipeline is based on the AgroLens repository (CVIMS, 2025)[16] and was updated to the Copernicus OData protocol, extended with feature transformation, and adapted for interactive use.

Feature transformation
13 additional predictor features were derived: 7 vegetation indices, 4 soil indices, and 2 cyclical temporal features encoding month of observation. This follows Nguyen et al. (2022)[6], where derived indices correlated more strongly with agricultural SOC than raw spectral bands, with SAVI identified as the single most important predictor.

Interactive use case
A fixed reference min-max normalization was implemented for both Sentinel-2 band values and all derived indices. Unlike dataset-dependent normalization, which scales values relative to the min/max of whichever dataset is being processed, the fixed approach computes reference min/max values once from a representative dataset and applies those constants consistently. This is required for the interactive use case where a farmer submits GPS coordinates and inference runs on a small location-specific file.

The fixed reference minimum and maximum values are computed once and saved to JSON. Each raw value \(x\) is transformed to \(x'=\frac{x-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}\) and then clipped to the unit interval: \(x' \leftarrow \min(1,\max(0,x'))\). If \(x_{\max}=x_{\min}\) the implementation returns 0 to avoid division by zero and NaN/inf values are replaced with 0 afterwards.

ML model

Selection, training, employment

The model architecture and implementation are based on Kammerlander (2025)[14], who evaluated XGBoost, Random Forest, and Fully Connected Neural Networks (FCNNs). The FCNN achieved the best overall performance and is well suited for learning nonlinear relationships in heterogeneous nutrient distributions.

FCNNs (multilayer perceptron) are feedforward neural networks in which each neuron of one layer is connected to all neurons of the subsequent layer. FCNNs are trained using backpropagation (Rumelhart, 1986)[17]. Through multiple hidden layers and nonlinear activation functions, FCNNs can learn complex nonlinear relationships between input features and target variables.

The network is constructed dynamically as a sequence of linear layers with ReLU activation and dropout after each hidden layer, followed by a single linear output neuron for predicting OC. Training is performed in PyTorch with mini-batches and RMSE as objective; weight decay of 0.01 is applied for regularization. Hyperparameter tuning is performed using Optuna (layers 3–9, neurons 8–128, dropout 0.1–0.5, learning rate 0.0001–0.01, optimizer SGD/Adam, batch size 16/32/64).

Using the described model architecture, a RMSE of 6.536 OC g/kg was obtained. This is relatively high, primarily due to the limited dataset size. The primary focus of the project was integration into a deployable pipeline and interactive web-based platform.

The prediction is implemented in run_pipeline_from_gps.py. Sentinel-2 imagery is downloaded, pixel values around each point are extracted, bands and derived features are normalized/computed, and the transformed dataset is passed to the neural network which outputs predicted carbon content (OC).

Model interaction
The pipeline is compiled into a wrapper script (run_pipeline_from_gps.py) and placed into the website backend. Users select their field; the backend then generates a fixed 60×60 m grid over the selected field polygon and uses the grid centers as analysis points. Output is visualized as a SOC map and summarized in the personal dashboard for easy access and repeated monitoring.

To speed up processing, the system reuses previously downloaded Sentinel scenes and cropped outputs whenever possible. Raw Sentinel scenes are cached as raw data, while cropped per-point outputs are stored together with metadata. To maximize cache reuse, the grid is dynamically aligned to existing cached points by selecting the anchor position with the highest overlap. If a cached point is found within a 10 m radius, the existing cropped image is reused instead of generating a new one. Only when no data is available, missing scenes are downloaded and cropped — significantly reducing processing time.

This caching mechanism can be extended to a background sync process that proactively downloads and updates cached scenes for a farm/region when new Sentinel acquisitions arrive, enabling near-instant analysis for farmers without waiting for on-demand downloads.

Website

Design objectives & interaction workflow

The AgroScan website was designed with the primary stakeholder in mind: a farmer without programming background but familiar with software-based farm management tools and an interest in carbon farming and sustainable soil management (SSM). Design objectives: usability/learnability/efficiency; long-term field monitoring; and meaningful technical agency over the model. These are addressed through a step-by-step workflow, a grid-style dashboard, and methodological transparency.

The core user flow follows a step-by-step navigation model. Several interaction design principles were applied: consistent “advance workflow” button styling, familiar icons, and error prevention via pop-ups and guided instructions.

ML explainability and information design
Prediction results are communicated through a map displaying spatial variability of SOC across the selected field. A color gradient from green (high SOC) to red (low SOC) provides intuitive encoding. Thresholds are based on the SOC distribution in the LUCAS dataset[15]. Technical agency is supported via CSV export, plain-language explanations, technical summaries, and the public GitHub repository.

Personal dashboard
The personal dashboard uses a grid layout to enable the farmer to quickly scan field-level information. Information is organized across two hierarchical levels: an overview of all registered fields (one tile per field) and a field detail view. Tile layout follows a left-to-right reading logic: field metadata first, then a line chart visualizing temporal development of SOC content alongside the latest SOC value, and finally a “Click to explore” button to access field details. The detail view provides higher resolution analysis information including minimum and maximum SOC values per analysis and a button to initiate a new analysis for the same field.

Ethical Considerations

Ethics of remote sensing

Remote sensing is widely used for environmental monitoring, agriculture, and climate research, but it also raises ethical concerns (Bennett et al., 2024)[7]. In AgroScan, two main concerns were identified: distance between data collection and affected communities, and limited transferability beyond Europe.

Distance & unintended impacts
Remote sensing creates a physical distance between data collection and affected communities. This may impose normative assumptions about SOC management without fully considering local knowledge, consent, and farming practices. While AgroScan can empower farmers to use sustainable soil management, it could also lead to unintended impacts such as institutions using outputs to impose and monitor regulations unsuited for the local context.

To address this, AgroScan is released as an open-source project with a detailed README and only publicly available datasets to reduce technical and financial barriers. Farmers can understand, reproduce, and adapt the methodology and use it to advocate in front of policymakers. Future releases aim to include community forums and local knowledge inputs (e.g., soil type, uploaded pictures) to strengthen participation and platform improvement.

Transferability beyond Europe
AgroScan has limited transferability beyond its European focus, shaped by available datasets and the team’s familiarity with German agricultural conditions. Applying the tool to different regional and social contexts may create unpredicted ethical risks (e.g., indigenous sovereignty, authoritarian misuse of agricultural information, or mismatches with local farming needs). Transferring AgroScan requires deep understanding of local contexts and ideally participatory engagement with local communities. In many lower-income regions, limited soil sampling data can create technical barriers, making participatory soil sampling campaigns important.

Evaluation

Limitations and next steps

AgroScan provides a proof of concept for a farmer-facing, interactive SOC monitoring platform. Limitations include a small training dataset, missing predictors (e.g., Sentinel-1), spatial resolution constraints, and globally-derived thresholds. Planned developments include retraining with LUCAS 2022, adding predictors, integrating farmer-provided inputs, and improving visualization thresholds.

The project addresses the central question through three channels: a simple step-by-step analysis workflow enabling routine SOC assessment; a grid-style dashboard conferring technical agency and enabling long-term comparison of SOC trends across fields to evaluate SSM practices; and an open-source architecture with publicly documented methodology closing transparency and accessibility gaps identified in the literature review.

Given the limited timeframe, model accuracy is constrained by the small dataset size. This can be improved by incorporating data from neighboring EU countries and retraining on the upcoming LUCAS 2022 release expected in Q3 2026. The current feature set omits predictors with established value (notably Sentinel-1 SAR), as well as crop yield, weather, and elevation data. Integrating farmer-provided local inputs such as soil type and bulk density could improve accuracy and bridge the physical separation inherent to remote sensing. Spatially, the current 60×60 m resolution limits within-field insights for smaller parcels; this could be reduced by leveraging full Sentinel-2 resolution and interpolating lower-res bands. Threshold classification could be made more regionally meaningful by incorporating location and soil type data.
